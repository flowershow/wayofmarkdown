# MARKDOWN IS EATING THE WORLDThere's a better way to do your notes, docs and site. It's plain text.
Written by Rufus Pollock.

Have you ever looked at what a note actually is? In most apps you can't: it's a row in someone else's database. A markdown note is a plain text file. A hash for a heading, stars for bold, a dash for a list. That's most of it, and AI already writes it for you.

It's already in more places than you've noticed: Google Docs, Obsidian, GitHub, Slack, every AI chat. Write a plain file and every tool you already use can read it. Change tools, keep the file.

Nobody cares about formats, any more than they care about TCP/IP. You care about what they let you do. Markdown is the rare case where the format made the difference, and it wasn't a straight line.

People could write it by hand, so tools took it as their format. Tools took it, so more people wrote it. The result: most of what you do in Notion, Google Docs or WordPress you can now do with markdown tools, combined how you like, keeping the files.

Start here

Three steps. None of them is a migration project.

  1. Learn the basics. Ten minutes.
  2. Pick a tool. Obsidian for notes, a publisher for a site.
  3. Move one thing. One folder, not your whole life.

Replacing Notion with Markdown

Still weighing it up? The scorecard is at Notion vs Markdown. This page is the how, for when you've decided.

Let me be clear upfront: Notion is genuinely good software. The blocks editor is slick, the databases are clever, and teams get real value from it. If it's working for you and the trade-offs below don't bother you, carry on.

But Notion is also a proprietary blocks database that owns your notes, your wiki and your little databases, rented back to you monthly. Your "pages" aren't files; they're rows in someone else's system, reachable only through their app and their API. And most people don't realise that most of what they use Notion for works in plain markdown files. Not with heroic effort. With a nice editor and a couple of conventions.

## 1The mapping

Take the things you actually do in Notion, one at a time:

In NotionIn markdownWith
Docs and notesa .md fileObsidian over a folder you own: live preview, slash commands, the lot.
Wikis[[wiki-links]]Obsidian for backlinks and graph; add GitHub for review and history.
DatabasesfrontmatterA folder of files is a table. Obsidian Bases gives views, filters, cards.
Public pagespublish the folderFlowershow or any static site tool. A real site on your domain.
Templates, tasks- [ ] built inObsidian has them, mostly built in, the rest a plugin away.

Databases are the interesting one, and the reason people think they're stuck. A Notion database is records with fields and views. But a markdown file with frontmatter is a record with fields:

A folder of these is a table. Here's one, three markdown files rendered as a live table in Obsidian:

And Obsidian Bases now does natively what Notion databases do: table views, filters, sorting, cards, all over your plain files. The full pattern, including when it's the wrong tool, is in The Markdown Database Pattern.

## 2What you give up, honestly

[ Not a free lunch ]

  1. Real-time multiplayer editing. Git-based collaboration is asynchronous: commit, push, review. Fine (better, even) for docs and knowledge; wrong for six people live-editing one meeting note.
  2. Granular permissions. A repo is shared or it isn't. Page-level "only Sarah can see this" needs separate repos or published subsets.
  3. Heavy relational databases. Thousands of records, rollups, complex relations: at that point you want an actual database, not files.

## 3The escape, step by step

  1. Export. Notion Settings → Export all workspace content → Markdown & CSV. You get a zip of markdown files and folders mirroring your workspace.
  2. Tidy. Notion appends ugly ID hashes to filenames; a rename pass fixes it.
  3. Open in Obsidian. Point it at the folder. That's it, you're operational.
  4. Add git when ready. Push the vault to GitHub for history, sync and collaboration.
  5. Publish if you want. Tutorial.

Notice what step 6 is: nothing. No subscription to start, no migration to schedule, because from here on your notes are just files.
