Identity first: the line, the feel and the logo, then one front page built from them.

Rufus's notes on draft 2: six options was too many, none of them landed, and the logo and feel should be settled before the site. Direction 1 was the closest, but it read cold, blue and like a technical manual. Direction 4 had the energy, and the little ASCII picture in its corner was the best detail in the brief.

So this draft has three logo options, not six directions, and one full front page that combines 1 with some of 4. The front page is warmed up with colour and flat graphics, and uses markdown-style headings. Motion is kept small throughout.

The line

Draft 2 said "the format unlocks an ecosystem", which was rightly met with "of what?". The answer is tools. The headline is agreed; the rest now says what the ecosystem is and why it holds together.

Headline
Markdown is eating the world.
Subline
One open format, and an extraordinary ecosystem of tools around it, from AI chat to your website. Because the format is open, the tools work together. Here's the way.
The idea
One open format. An ecosystem of tools that compose.The Unix idea, applied to documents: small tools that each do one thing well, with plain text between them. Write in one tool, publish with another, let an AI edit it in a third. "Compose with anything" was trying to say this; it needed the Unix story to land.
Sign-off
Openness is the way.Footer line and the manifesto's closing line. Plays on the name without being cute about it. "Own the source" stays inside the manifesto as an argument, not a slogan.
Feel

Warm, clear, a bit geeky, open. Made by a person who is excited about this.

Like

Notion's friendliness, Wikipedia's openness, Making Software's clarity, a poster's confidence in the headline.

Not

A technical manual, a terminal, a SaaS landing page, a monastery, anything elaborate for its own sake.

The logo

Three options, each with a single small motion. The mark has to hold at 16 px as a favicon, and work as the heading marker across the site.

The Way of Markdown

A. The brush hash recommended

Markdown's most recognisable character, written with a brush. The # says markdown, and the brush says "the way": a practice, made by hand. Four strokes with a loaded start and a dry tail.

Motion: it writes itself, stroke by stroke, once on load. The same glyph becomes every heading marker on the site, so the logo is everywhere without being repeated.

#*>-
The Way of Markdown

B. The blocks

Four markdown characters as coloured tiles: heading, emphasis, quote, list. They are the building blocks, and they rearrange into a line, a column and a square. Composition made visible.

Risk: friendly and colourful, but tile logos are common, and at 16 px it is four coloured squares with no markdown left in it.

The Way of Markdown

C. The practitioner

The existing ink tai chi figure, pressing out of an orange sun. It's human and warm, and it is the only option that says "way".

Risk: it says nothing about markdown, and at 16 px it is an orange dot. It is better as a character on the site than as the logo: it could appear in the hero, on the 404 page and in the press kit.

Why A: it is the only one that says markdown and craft in a single shape, it reads at 16 px, and it gives the site a system rather than a badge, because every heading carries it. The tai chi figure stays as the site's character (option C's role), so the August work is kept. The blocks are the most fun of the three but the least ownable.

Side by side

The same hero in each of the three identities, so the logo choice can be judged on a page and not only as a badge. Same paper, same type, same words. Only the mark, its colour and its one graphic change.

One open format

An extraordinary ecosystem of tools, and they compose.

Markdown is plain text with a few conventions: a # for a heading, a * for emphasis, a > for a quote. That's it. Because it's open, everyone implements it, so every tool can read the same file.

This is the old Unix idea applied to documents: small tools that each do one thing well, with plain text passed between them. Write in Obsidian, publish with any static site tool, keep history in git, hand it to an AI. Swap any of them out tomorrow and the file doesn't notice.

Plain text never goes out of fashion.

Why it works

Easy to publish. Yours to keep. Grows when you need it to.

Most tools give you one or two of these. A hosted platform is easy to publish on, but you don't control your content. A pile of text files is yours, but it's hard to publish. A database grows, but it isn't easy and it isn't yours.

Markdown, with the right tools around it, sits in the middle of all three. That middle used to be empty.

Start here

Learn the syntaxTen minutes. There's honestly not much of it. Pick your toolsAn editor, somewhere to keep the files, a way to publish. All swappable. Build your thingA site, a garden, a database. Follow the roadmap at your own pace.
